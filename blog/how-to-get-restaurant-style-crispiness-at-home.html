How to Get Restaurant-Style Crispiness in Your Home Cooking

The crunch you get at a restaurant isn't luck. It's a handful of frying habits. Here are the ones that matter.

RS Chef'z Editorial Team14 September 2026 3 min read

You follow the recipe, you fry the starter, and somehow it comes out softer and greasier than the same dish at your local restaurant. It's the most common home-cooking frustration there is, and it's almost never the recipe's fault. Restaurant crispiness comes from a few repeatable habits, not from special equipment.

Here's what actually makes the difference, whether you're frying Gobi Manchurian, Chicken 65, fish or pakoras.

1. Dry Ingredients Fry Crisp; Wet Ones Steam

Moisture is the enemy of crunch. Water on the surface of a vegetable or a piece of chicken turns to steam the moment it hits the oil, and steam softens the coating from the outside in. Pat everything completely dry before it's battered, and drain parboiled vegetables thoroughly. This one habit fixes half of all soggy-fry problems.

2. Cornflour and Rice Flour Are Your Friends

Plain flour alone makes a bready, heavy coating. The crisp, glassy crust you get at restaurants comes from adding cornflour (cornstarch) and, for fried meat and fish, a little rice flour or fine semolina. They fry up lighter and stay crisp longer. A good ratio is roughly two parts plain flour to one part cornflour for battered vegetables.

Keep the batter thick

A thin batter runs off and fries patchy. Aim for a batter that coats the back of a spoon and clings to the ingredient, thick enough to build a shell, thin enough to stay light.

3. Get the Oil Temperature Right

This is the big one. Oil that's too cool soaks into the coating instead of crisping it; oil that's too hot burns the outside before the inside cooks. The sweet spot for most starters is around 180°C. No thermometer? Drop in a little batter, it should sizzle and rise steadily to the surface within a couple of seconds, neither sinking nor browning instantly.

4. Never Crowd the Pan

Every piece you add cools the oil down. Pile in too many and the temperature crashes, the coating goes greasy, and everything sticks together. Fry in small batches, give each piece room, and let the oil come back up to heat between rounds. It feels slower, but it's the single biggest visible difference between home and restaurant frying.

5. Double-Fry for Serious Crunch

The professional trick for extra-crisp results is a double fry: fry once until just cooked, rest for a few minutes, then fry again briefly at a slightly higher heat just before serving. The first fry cooks the inside; the second drives off surface moisture and locks in the crunch. It's how restaurants keep things crisp even under a sauce.

6. Sauce and Serve at the Last Minute

Crispiness is fragile. If you toss fried food in sauce and let it sit, it softens fast. Sauce over high heat for under a minute, right before serving, and bring the plate to the table straight away. For dry starters, a wire rack (not a covered bowl) keeps steam from collecting and going soft.

7. Season Well, and Season Consistently

Crunch without flavour is just texture. The coating needs to be properly seasoned so the taste runs through the whole bite, not just the surface. This is where a balanced masala earns its keep, it seasons evenly every time, so you're not guessing at spice ratios while also managing hot oil.
